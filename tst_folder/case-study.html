The problem

Every free stock screener lies by omission

Retail screeners hand you a number. What they don't tell you is how much of that number rests on data that was missing, stale, or contradicted by another source.

A company with negative equity and a broken balance sheet can still post a beautiful momentum reading. A company whose last filing is two years old still gets a P/E printed next to its name, computed from figures nobody has verified since. The screener shows 82/100 either way, and the number carries no memory of how it was made.

I wanted the opposite: an engine where data quality is a first-class input to the score, not a footnote. If the inputs are weak, the output must be visibly, structurally capped — not quietly optimistic.

The engine

How the score is built

Every company gets a 0–100 score. It is a weighted mean of six measured components, plus a deliberate seventh term, minus a risk penalty — and then passed through a set of hard ceilings.

23% 20% 15% 14% 12% 8% 8%
Quality23%

Net and EBIT margins, ROE from SEC filings. ROE distorted by a near-zero equity base is discarded rather than rewarded.

Financial health20%

Debt/Equity, Net Debt/EBITDA, interest coverage, equity ratio. Net cash earns a bonus.

Momentum15%

Price vs MA50/MA200, RSI, position in the 52-week range, 1-year return. Parabolic moves are capped, not extrapolated.

Valuation14%

P/E, P/B, P/S, EV/EBITDA, P/FCF, FCF yield. REITs switch to P/FFO, because P/E is meaningless for them.

Growth12%

Revenue growth. Anomalies beyond +100% or −50% are ignored — an accounting artifact is not a growth story.

Dividend8%

Yield computed from actual payments in the trailing 12 months, plus payout sustainability.

Neutral anchor8%

A fixed 50. Not a measurement — a damper, so no single component can drag the composite to an extreme.

score = 0.23·quality + 0.20·health + 0.15·momentum + 0.14·valuation + 0.12·growth + 0.08·dividend + 0.08·50 − risk_penalty

The neutral anchor is the interesting part

Six components at full weight produce a bimodal distribution: everything clusters at the extremes, because one strong signal drags the whole composite with it. The fixed 50 at 8% acts as a regularizer — it pulls every score toward the middle by a known, constant amount, so the difference between a 71 and an 84 reflects broad agreement across components rather than one component shouting.

It costs a small amount of dynamic range and buys back a score that behaves monotonically. That trade was worth making.

Integrity

Hard ceilings: momentum can't paper over a broken balance sheet

Weighted means have a failure mode. A stock in a violent uptrend scores high on momentum and cheap on valuation, and those two together can carry a company with negative equity into the 70s. That's exactly the recommendation that loses people money.

So after the weighted mean, the score passes through ceilings. These are not penalties that get averaged in — they are caps applied last, and they cannot be outvoted.

ConditionScore ceilingWhy
Financial health < 3557A shaky balance sheet caps the whole thesis, whatever the chart says.
Financial health < 2247Distress territory. Nothing else in the model is allowed to override it.
Negative equity, or invalid market cap / price45The valuation inputs are not merely bad, they are undefined.
Fundamentals older than 18 months55The figures may be accurate and still describe a company that no longer exists.
Unverified one-off growth, or P/E disagreeing across sources60Two sources contradicting each other is information — treat it as such.

Confidence is separate from score

Alongside the score, each company carries a confidence value measuring how complete its inputs were: is SEC data present, is the P/E uncontested across sources, is anything stale. Below a threshold the score is labelled uncertain in the interface rather than silently presented as equivalent to a fully-sourced one.

Design rule

Missing data is displayed as N/A, never imputed and never penalised as if it were bad news. Earnings surprises, forward guidance and analyst revisions don't exist in free data sources — so the engine says so, instead of inventing a plausible number. A model that quietly fills gaps teaches you to trust it exactly where it deserves it least.

From score to sentence

The number is the intermediate representation, not the product. The output is a verdict in plain language, with the reasons that produced it.

≥ 75Strong candidate for analysis — requires sufficient data and no red flags
≥ 60Worth analysing, check the risks — downgraded to “Mixed” if the balance sheet is weak
≥ 45Neutral — watchlist only
< 45Elevated risk — avoid until clarified

Architecture

One PHP file, no database, no build step

The backend is a single api.php: 86 functions behind 14 endpoints, running on ordinary shared hosting. The frontend is one vanilla JS file driving a hash-routed SPA. There is no framework, no bundler, no ORM, and no database.

That was a decision, not a shortcut. The workload is fetch from three flaky upstream APIs, normalise wildly inconsistent shapes, compute, cache to disk. A database adds a schema migration problem to a system whose canonical data lives upstream anyway; a framework adds boot cost to a request that is already latency-bound on outbound HTTP.

01Universe

A static 1,538-row manifest — S&P 500 constituents with GICS sector and SEC CIK, plus manually curated European and US REITs and international ADRs.

02Fetch

curl_multi issues parallel requests to SEC EDGAR companyfacts and Yahoo Finance, sharing one disk cache keyed by SHA-1 of the URL.

03Normalise

SEC is authoritative where a CIK exists. Non-US issuers fall back to Yahoo quoteSummary. Share counts, market cap and valuation multiples get reconciled across sources.

04Score

Components, risk penalty, ceilings, confidence, verdict — then ranked by whichever of the 14 strategies is active.

Two sources, one truth

SEC EDGAR is the primary fundamentals source and it is excellent — official, structured, free. It also only covers US registrants. Roughly 20 companies in the universe are non-US issuers with no usable CIK, and for those the engine falls back to Yahoo's quoteSummary.

The two disagree more often than you'd hope. Share counts differ across reporting dates, net income gets extracted from a different line item, market cap drifts. Rather than pick a winner globally, the engine reconciles per-metric and, where the disagreement is material, treats it as a data-quality signal — the P/E-conflict ceiling above exists precisely for this case.

Constraints

Fitting a whole-universe scan into shared hosting

The most instructive engineering problem in this project had nothing to do with finance.

Scoring the full universe means holding decompressed SEC companyfacts documents in memory — some are tens of megabytes each. The first working version did the obvious thing: fetch everything in parallel, then score. On my machine it was fast. On the live host it returned zero results, with no error.

The process was being OOM-killed mid-scan. Because the failure happened inside a batch that never returned, the symptom presented as an empty result set rather than a crash — which is precisely the kind of bug that sends you hunting through scoring logic for a day while the actual cause is the host's memory_limit.

The fix was structural: process the pool in chunks of 25 symbols, resolve market caps per batch, and release each batch's parsed documents before the next one is fetched, so peak memory stays flat regardless of universe size rather than scaling with it.

LayerTTLReasoning
SEC / Yahoo fundamentals24 hFilings change quarterly. Refetching hourly buys nothing and risks rate limits.
Price history (OHLCV)15 minMoving averages and RSI don't move meaningfully inside a quarter-hour.
Quotes60 sFresh enough to feel live, coarse enough to stay within upstream limits.

A cold run warms the 24-hour cache and is slow by design; every subsequent run is fast. Cache is plain files on disk, keyed by SHA-1 of the request URL, with a manual purge exposed in the UI for when a fresh filing lands.

Strategies

Fourteen ways to ask the same data a different question

The components are computed once. A strategy is a re-weighting plus a filter — which keeps them all consistent with one another and with the single-company detail view. Two are worth describing in full.

Future Market Leaders

A gate before a score. A company must clear every hard filter — revenue growth above 20% year-over-year, trading above both MA50 and MA200, MA50 above MA200, trend rising, and not over-extended — before it is scored at all. Survivors get a dedicated 0–100 composite: growth 40%, trend and relative strength 25%, quality 20%, balance sheet 15%. The filter does the work; the score only ranks what's left.

Fair value

Intrinsic value is estimated three independent ways — a normalised P/E, a free-cash-flow reversion, and EV/EBITDA — and the engine takes the median, not the mean. One broken input can't drag the estimate; it just loses the vote. Only profitable companies with a genuine margin of safety against current price are surfaced as undervalued.

Supply chains

Sector labels are close to useless for thematic investing — a chip-packaging supplier and a consumer laptop brand both file under Information Technology. So chains (semiconductors, AI & data centre, pharma, energy) are a hand-curated catalogue mapping companies to their role in a value chain, independent of GICS classification.

The technical setups — pre-golden-cross, trend repair, quality pullback, breakout confirmation, earnings acceleration — each run an explicit step-by-step checklist and emit a staged verdict (WATCH → ACCUMULATE → BUY CONFIRMED → INVALID SETUP), so the user sees which condition passed and which one is still missing, rather than a bare signal.

Assessment

What I'd change

Three things are structurally wrong with it, and none of them are secrets.

  • Hash routing. Eight views share one URL. It was the right call for a tool people operate; it's the wrong call for content anyone might find. Real paths would turn one indexable page into eight.
  • Weights are asserted, not fitted. 23/20/15/14/12/8 comes from domain reasoning, not backtesting. Defensible for an educational tool that shows its work; not defensible if the claim were predictive accuracy. Backtesting against forward returns is the honest next step — and would probably move several of those numbers.
  • Free data has a floor. No earnings surprises, no guidance, no analyst revisions, no intraday depth. European REIT fundamentals are largely absent, leaving price, trend and yield to carry the score. The engine is explicit about this everywhere it matters, but explicitness isn't coverage.

The thing I'd keep unchanged is the ceiling mechanism. Every time I've been tempted to soften a cap so a company I liked could score higher, the cap was right and I was wrong.
